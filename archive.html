An archival processing project focused on organizing, describing, preserving, and improving access to historical records of the International Book Project (IBP), a Lexington, Kentucky nonprofit organization founded in 1966.

Project Overview

The International Book Project Records document more than five decades of nonprofit activity, including book donation and distribution programs, fundraising, organizational development, international partnerships, and community engagement.

As part of an independent archival project, I conducted an initial inventory and developed a processing plan for a heterogeneous collection containing correspondence, administrative records, photographs, publications, financial records, scrapbooks, and audiovisual materials.

The project focused on balancing archival principles with the practical needs and resources of a small nonprofit organization.

Arrangement

The existing collection had no consistent overarching arrangement. Many folders contained mixed record types, while some materials retained partial chronological or topical groupings.

After consultation with the organization’s director, rearrangement into functional series was permitted in order to improve intellectual access and structural clarity.

Series I — Correspondence Organizational and individual correspondence.
Series II — Newsletters Organizational newsletters dating from 1973–2002.
Series III — Newspaper & Periodical Coverage Public coverage of IBP and related activities.
Series IV — Administrative Records Meeting minutes, board records, and annual reports.
Series V — Photographic Materials Photographs documenting IBP activities, volunteers, recipients, and founder Harriet Van Meter.
Series VI — Financial Records Financial reports and related fiscal records.
Series VII — Outreach & Fundraising Events, fundraising, and public engagement materials.
Series VIII — Founder Personal Files Personal correspondence and materials related to Harriet Van Meter.

Processing Decisions

Existing Order

Rather than imposing a new arrangement without consultation, I evaluated existing groupings and discussed proposed rearrangement with the organization’s director.

Resource-Aware Processing

Processing decisions considered the organization’s available resources, existing housing, and practical access needs rather than assuming a museum-scale infrastructure.

Preservation

Rusted paper clips were identified for removal and records were planned for rehousing in acid-free folders.

Faded Materials

Two thermal-paper documents were highly faded. Digitization was prioritized to preserve access to information that was becoming difficult to read.

Digitization

Digitization was planned as an integral part of the processing workflow rather than as a separate activity. Digital files were organized according to the established archival structure and assigned standardized filenames.

The collection included multiple legacy formats requiring different approaches to digitization. VHS and U-matic materials presented additional challenges because digitization requires real-time playback and specialized equipment.

Slide Digitization

Historical slides were digitized in batches as part of the project’s audiovisual preservation workflow.

VHS Digitization

VHS recordings were processed through a real-time digitization workflow, with attention to file management and preservation.

Access note: Digitized collection materials are not publicly displayed in this portfolio because the collection is restricted to internal organizational access. This page documents the preservation and digitization workflow rather than reproducing restricted collection content.

Finding Aid

A finding aid was developed to provide intellectual access to the processed collection. The description includes collection-level information, administrative history, scope and content, arrangement, and folder-level descriptions.

Access note: The finding aid is currently maintained for internal organizational use and is not publicly distributed through this portfolio.

Reflection

This project reinforced that archival practice requires balancing professional standards with the context and resources of the organization.

Working with a heterogeneous collection also demonstrated how arrangement and description directly influence discovery and access. Decisions about organization, metadata, preservation, and digitization are interconnected rather than isolated stages of processing.

Most importantly, the project allowed me to connect archival theory with hands-on processing and to develop practical approaches for making community collections more usable and sustainable.
